# MathWorks Software Implementation

## What problem are we focusing on?

When our team first visited the Añú community in Santa Rosa de Agua, we were struck by a profound generational contrast. While elderly artisans and musicians demonstrated extraordinary manual dexterity playing traditional instruments, younger generations were experiencing a silent health crisis: an estimated 18% decline in fine motor skills and neuromuscular coordination driven by passive digital screen consumption, alongside a 95% loss of their ancestral cultural heritage. 

Addressing a dual challenge of this magnitude required a radical rethinking of rehabilitation technology. In conventional clinical practice, assessing fine motor impairments and quantifying pathological tremors requires strapping expensive Inertial Measurement Units, bulky mechanical exoskeletons, or clinical electromyography (EMG) electrodes onto a patient's hand. While effective in isolated hospital laboratories, these invasive setups intimidate young patients, restrict natural hand movement, and suffer from high therapy abandonment rates due to monotonous mechanical repetition The OMS demonstrates that playing an instrument and rhythmic-auditory training activate cerebral sensorimotor pathways, significantly improving upper-limb control, manual dexterity, and fine motor precision in children and young people, [as you can see in this study](https://github.com/anuwarobotics/Anuwa-robotics-/blob/main/Resources/OMS_Study.pdf).

Our MathWorks software implementation introduces a highly innovative paradigm in assistive robotics and biomechanical telemetry: **we transform a traditional wind instrument into a non invasive, autonomous clinical diagnostic device using capacitive touch sensing.** Rather than forcing a patient to wear medical hardware, our system monitors the human hand organically as the user plays ancestral melodies on our modified flute unit *Joroi*. By capturing micro-variations across six built in capacitive copper spirals and processing them through a **Simulink** environment, we turn an act of cultural preservation into precision occupational therapy.

## What the MathWorks System Does

At the core of the Anuwa Robotics system, the MathWorks software architecture acts as both the autonomous clinical diagnostician and the real-time orchestrator of the rehabilitation workflow. Without requiring manual calibration or subjective human observation, the system performs three critical functions simultaneously:

### 1. Non Invasive Capacitive Biometric Telemetry
As the user places their fingers over the six note pads of the flute, the system continuously captures the raw capacitive touch signals generated by the human body's natural conductivity. Every micro-fluctuation in finger stability, contact duration, and transitional agility is treated not merely as a musical input, but as a continuous biometric data stream reflecting the patient's neuromuscular control.

### 2. Real-Time Spectral Analysis Across the 0 to 12 Hz Band
Human hand movements both voluntary and involuntary carry distinct frequency signatures. As the user plays, the MathWorks environment continuously analyzes the rate of change in finger contact across the **0 to 12 Hz frequency spectrum**. Instead of simply checking whether a musical note was pressed or missed, the software evaluates the physical stability of the fingers, isolating the three most dominant oscillation frequencies occurring during the session alongside the overall movement intensity.

### 3. Autonomous  Classification (0–4 Hz vs. 4–12 Hz)
Based on the isolated dominant frequencies, the system autonomously classifies the user's motor profile in real time and adapts the entire robotic environment accordingly:
* **Voluntary & Physiological Motion (0 to 3.5 Hz):** When the user's dominant finger oscillations remain within the lower spectrum—specifically around the **0 to 4 Hz** range—the software identifies healthy, voluntary motor coordination and normal physiological micro-tremors. In response, the system rewards therapeutic adherence, advances the clinical score, and triggers synchronized robotic accompaniment across our percussion and humanoid units.
* **Pathological Tremor & Fine Motor Impairment (4 to 12 Hz):** When the software detects dominant oscillatory peaks within the **4 to 12 Hz** band, it autonomously classifies the behavior as a fine motor anomaly or tremor profile. Without human intervention, the system flags the clinical telemetry gauge into the high-risk alert zone, quantifies the severity of the motor instability, and dynamically adjusts the gamified scoring feedback to reflect omission streaks or involuntary ghost touches.

## Why MathWorks software?

A central engineering question in our project was deciding where and how to design, verify, and execute this clinical logic. Developing a medical-grade rehabilitation system is fundamentally different from building a standard hobbyist robot; it demands mathematical rigor, timing determinism, and verifiable reliability. 

### Why Not Hand-Coded C/C++ Directly on the ESP32?
Our physical instrument relies on an ESP32 microcontroller for low-level hardware interfacing. However, attempting to write, tune, and debug complex finite state machines and digital signal processing pipelines directly in bare-metal C++ on the ESP32 presented severe engineering bottlenecks:

1. **Computational limitations of Edge computing:** Designing high-order digital filters and frequency-domain transformations from scratch on an embedded microcontroller requires  fixed-point or floating-point approximations. A single miscalculation in raw C code can introduce phase lag or spectral leakage, resulting in false clinical diagnoses.
2. **Lack of Pre-Deployment Patient Validation:** Directly flashing experimental C++ code onto hardware in a rural community like Santa Rosa de Agua without prior mathematical verification is clinically irresponsible. We needed a way to simulate both healthy users and patients with severe motor tremors before ever touching a physical microcontroller.


### The MathWorks Advantage: Model Based Design
Choosing **MATLAB, Simulink, Stateflow, DSP System Toolbox, Simulink Coder, and Embedded Coder** allowed our team to adopt an industry standard **Model-Based Design** workflow:
* **Unified Visual Architecture:** Simulink and Stateflow allowed us to visually architect the entire therapeutic state machine and signal analysis pipeline in a single, self-documenting environment, eliminating the ambiguity of thousands of lines of raw code.
* **Interactive Clinical Simulation:** Before deploying to the field, MathWorks enabled us to build an interactive offline testing harness capable of synthesizing multi-channel patient profiles allowing us to mathematically prove that our system distinguishes a healthy 1.5 Hz voluntary movement from a 6.5 Hz pathological tremor.
* **Verified Automated Code Generation:** Rather than writing error prone firmware by hand, we used **Embedded Coder** to automatically compile our verified Stateflow logic directly into a clean, highly optimized, and deterministic **ISO C++03** library (`.h` and `.cpp`) that runs natively on the ESP32.

## Scientific & Clinical Justification

Every frequency threshold, diagnostic boundary, and classification rule implemented in our MathWorks environment is strictly backed by peer-reviewed neurological and clinical research indexed in **PubMed Central (PMC)**:

1. **Sensor-Based Quantification of 4–12 Hz Essential Tremors:**  
   In the landmark IEEE/NIH study [**"Quantifying Tremor in Essential Tremor Using Inertial Sensors—Validation of an Algorithm"**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7608862/) (*McGurrin et al., 2021*), researchers demonstrated that essential tremor—the most prevalent movement disorder affecting upper-limb fine motor control—occurs specifically within the **4 to 12 Hz** frequency window. Their findings validate that algorithmic spectral analysis of sensor data accurately quantifies tremor frequency and amplitude within this exact 4–12 Hz band, providing objective metrics that match clinical rating scales without requiring a specialist's visual estimation.

2. **Neurological Differentiation Across the 0–12 Hz Spectrum:**  
   According to the clinical framework established in [**"Tremor Pathophysiology"**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13235525/), involuntary rhythmic oscillations of the extremities are clinically classified by their spectral behavior. The literature confirms that while normal voluntary coordination and benign physiological oscillations remain anchored in the lower frequency band, central neurogenic and pathological motor disorders manifest as distinct spectral peaks between **4 and 12 Hz**. This directly justifies our system's 12 Hz upper analysis limit and our 4 Hz autonomous classification boundary.

3. **Clinical Assessment of Finger Dexterity and Action Tremors:**  
   As detailed in [**"The Clinical Evaluation of Parkinson's Tremor"**](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4923747/), tremors affecting the hands and fingers typically present between **4 and 6 Hz** at rest and extend up to **9–12 Hz** during postural holding and active kinetic tasks—such as holding and playing a wind instrument. Continuously monitoring this specific frequency band during active finger manipulation provides a clinically valid foundation for early detection, progress tracking, and targeted physical rehabilitation.
